Title: Comment on Considerations for the Regulation of Generative AI-Enabled Medical Devices (Docket FDA-2026-N-7874)<br/><br/>Target Question: Discussion Question 24 &ndash; Detection, Evaluation, and Response to Third-Party Model Updates<br/><br/>Executive Summary<br/>As Generative AI (GenAI) and foundation models are integrated into medical devices, managing risk around unannounced upstream updates by third-party API providers represents a critical regulatory and operational hurdle. While static software components follow traditional change-control protocols, hosted foundation models often undergo silent weight adjustments or fine-tuning updates.<br/><br/>To ensure continuous safety, effectiveness, and auditability, manufacturers must employ combination strategies involving localized model locking, strict QMS version lifecycle management, and continuous automated drift detection.<br/><br/>Key Recommendations &amp; Technical Approaches<br/>1. On-Device &amp; Private Cloud Model Locking (Mitigation of Silent Updates)<br/>Local SLM Architecture: Where clinical utility permits, manufacturers should deploy domain-specific Small Language Models (SLMs) locally on-device or within private, locked environments.<br/><br/>Control of Update Cycles: Local hosting isolates the medical device from unannounced third-party API changes, ensuring that model weight updates occur exclusively through controlled, validated manufacturer release cycles.<br/><br/>2. Managing Multi-Version Lifecycle Proliferation in the QMS<br/>Version Support Matrix: Recognizing that healthcare facilities and clinical sites upgrade software asynchronously, local model locking shifts the operational burden to multi-version lifecycle management.<br/><br/>Quality System Expectations: The FDA should encourage manufacturers to incorporate strict backward-compatibility testing matrices, automated re-validation suites, and clear deprecation schedules directly into their Quality Management System (QMS) to support multiple active field baselines transparently during audits.<br/><br/>3. Automated Detection Protocols for Hosted Third-Party APIs<br/>When reliance on hosted third-party foundation model APIs is unavoidable, manufacturers must establish proactive detection mechanisms to catch upstream model behavior shifts before they impact clinical care:<br/><br/>Golden Synthetic Benchmark Suites: Automated CI/CD pipelines running fixed, representative clinical benchmark datasets (prompts/inputs paired with expected standard outputs) on a regular cadence (e.g., daily/weekly) to flag output drift beyond clinical tolerance thresholds.<br/><br/>Semantic Drift Analysis: Embeddings-based tracking of production inputs and outputs to measure statistical similarity over time, catching subtle reasoning shifts or vocabulary changes.<br/><br/>Guardrail &amp; Validator Telemetry: Real-time monitoring of post-processing guardrail trigger rates and rule-based validation failures to signal unexpected changes in model behavior.<br/><br/>Suggested FDA Guidance Action<br/>The FDA should provide clear regulatory frameworks that:<br/><br/>Recognize localized version locking (e.g., on-edge SLMs) as an acceptable risk-mitigation strategy for change control under Premarket Approval (PMA) or 510(k) modifications.<br/><br/>Define acceptable validation standards for continuous automated benchmarking when hosted third-party APIs are utilized.<br/><br/>Establish clear documentation expectations for supporting multi-version active deployments across fragmented clinical sites.
