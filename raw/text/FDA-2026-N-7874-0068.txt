Thank you to the FDA and Dr. Tarver for inviting public input on how AI should be evaluated and used in medicine. I strongly support making medical AI safe, scientifically responsible, and useful to patients and clinicians.<br/><br/>I ask the FDA to make an important principle part of medical AI development: AI should search for and evaluate the full body of credible medical evidence globally and impartially&mdash;not rely predominantly on conventional U.S. pharmaceutical and medical literature.<br/><br/>This should include conventional medicine, complementary and traditional medicine, acupuncture, homeopathy, herbal medicine, nutritional therapies, and naturally derived substances studied in Europe, the Middle East, Asia, India, and elsewhere.<br/><br/>This does not mean AI should assume these treatments work. Quite the opposite. AI should evaluate them by the same rigorous standards applied to conventional treatments.<br/><br/>AI should be able to ask:<br/><br/>What clinical studies have been conducted?<br/>Were they randomized, controlled, blinded, and adequately powered?<br/>Have results been replicated?<br/>What are the risks, side effects, contraindications, and drug interactions?<br/>What credible evidence exists internationally?<br/>Where is evidence strong, weak, conflicting, or insufficient?<br/>Which promising treatments deserve further research?<br/><br/>For example, black cumin seed (Nigella sativa) oil, long used in Middle Eastern medicine, has been investigated in randomized clinical studies and systematic reviews for possible effects on blood glucose and other metabolic measures.<br/><br/>Another example is Lion&rsquo;s Mane mushroom (Hericium erinaceus). A double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial investigated its effects in people with mild cognitive impairment and reported improved cognitive scores during treatment.<br/><br/>Homeopathy and acupuncture likewise should not simply be dismissed because they fall outside conventional pharmaceutical medicine. They should be examined objectively: find the underlying studies, evaluate their methodology, compare the results with other treatments, and determine what the evidence actually supports.<br/><br/>I therefore encourage the FDA to consider medical AI standards that include:<br/><br/>Broad evidence retrieval beyond a limited set of conventional U.S. databases.<br/>International evidence, including credible research from Europe, the Middle East, Asia, India, and elsewhere.<br/>Consistent evidence grading, distinguishing anecdotal reports, laboratory and animal studies, observational studies, randomized trials, systematic reviews, and replicated clinical evidence.<br/>Fair comparative evaluation of conventional, complementary, and traditional treatments using the same standards.<br/>Transparency, allowing patients and clinicians to understand the evidence and uncertainties behind an AI recommendation.<br/>Identification of research gaps, so potentially promising treatments are identified for further properly designed research rather than simply excluded.<br/><br/>The FDA&#39;s effort to establish standards for medical AI is an extraordinary opportunity. Rather than teaching AI to reproduce only the limitations of today&#39;s medical system, we should use it to examine the world&#39;s medical knowledge systematically and scientifically.<br/><br/>Let AI investigate broadly. Let the evidence determine the conclusion.<br/><br/>Personal reason for my comment: A diabetic friend whose eyesight had become severely impaired began taking approximately one tablespoon of Nigella (black seed) oil nightly. Three years later, he reported that his eyesight had greatly improved and that he was no longer considered diabetic. His doctor told him to continue the black seed oil. I recognize that this is only an anecdotal experience, not scientific proof. But it illustrates why I believe AI should be able to find and objectively evaluate treatments that patients may otherwise never learn about.<br/><br/>Thank you for considering this perspective.<br/><br/>Linda Ahrens
