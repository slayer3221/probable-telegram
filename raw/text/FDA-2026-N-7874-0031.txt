Who are we going to blame?<br/><br/>AI naysayers often ask this contentious question about AI use in medicine. If there is no doctor in the loop, who are we going to hold liable for mistakes?<br/><br/>Given that today, we hold humans liable, how is that working out for the nearly 800,000 people who die and become permanently disabled from human errors every year?<br/><br/>Besides, as with any commercial product, we will hold the manufacturer, distributor, etc. liable for any failures.<br/><br/>Here are the facts: Manufacturers and sellers have faced basic negligence and contract rules for centuries, but modern product liability holding entire supply chains accountable for product failures evolved in two major legal leaps: the elimination of the &quot;privity&quot; requirement in 1916 and the rise of &quot;strict liability&quot; in the 1960s.  <br/><br/>How it was: For a long time, courts used the &quot;privity of contract&quot; rule (bolstered by cases like the 1842 English case Winterbottom v. Wright). This meant a consumer could only sue the exact person they bought the item from, leaving them unable to sue distant factory makers. <br/><br/>What changed? The landmark New York case MacPherson v. Buick Motor Co. (1916) discarded the privity barrier for negligence. It set the rule that makers owe a direct duty of care to ultimate users, even if they did not buy the product straight from the factory. <br/><br/>The 1960s Revolution (Strict Liability)<br/><br/>The Old Rule: Proving a factory or inventor was careless (negligent) or broke a specific sales contract remained difficult for everyday injured users. <br/><br/>The Shift: During the 1960s, courts and legal scholars shifted to &quot;strict liability&quot;. Under this doctrine (formalized in Section 402A of the Restatement (Second) of Torts in 1965), an injured user no longer had to prove negligence. If a product left the maker with an unreasonably dangerous defect and caused harm, the business was liable. <br/><br/>Doctors are not held to this standard. So AI will be held to a higher standard.<br/><br/>Distributors/Suppliers: As part of the 1960s expansion, courts applied the &quot;stream of commerce&quot; concept. Wholesalers, distributors, and retailers became strictly liable for placing defective goods on the market, sparing consumers from tracking down a remote or overseas factory. <br/><br/>Hospitals and medical clinics are not held to these standards. Again, AI will be held to a higher standard.<br/><br/>Inventors/Designers: Designers and patent holders face liability if a failure stems from a flawed blueprint or unsafe concept rather than a factory assembly error. <br/><br/>Medical association guidelines and researchers are not held to these standards.<br/><br/>But here&#39;s the real point. AI will make far fewer mistakes than humans. Far fewer. Perhaps none at all? Then no one will have to hold anybody to blame.<br/><br/>How about that?<br/><br/>Give AI room to breathe and let market forces reign it in.<br/>
